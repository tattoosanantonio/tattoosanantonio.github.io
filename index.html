    Pick the style first, then the artist

    The fastest way to narrow twenty names is to decide what the tattoo should look like. Each style below points to the artists in this list who say they do it.

    Realism and portraits. Likenesses of people and pets need an artist who is comfortable with shading and detail at size. Abraham Yepes lists portraits specifically, Minh Pham lists realism alongside anime and fine line, Aaron Torres works in black and grey realism, and Josh Herrera adds realism to a very wide range of styles.

    Black and grey. Without color, the shading carries the whole piece, so check how smooth the gradients look once healed. Aaron Torres focuses on black and grey realism, Abraham Yepes and Minh Pham both list it next to color work, and Josh Herrera, Kristen (florals only) and Sean round out the group.

    Fine line and florals. Delicate lines suit lettering and plants, and they soften with time, so ask any fine line artist how a piece will look in five years. Yvette covers fine line and script, Kristen sticks to black and grey florals, and Geo and Isabella Almaleh lean toward botanical and nature subjects.

    Anime. Anime tattoos live or die on clean outlines and solid color, so look for portfolios that show both. Minh Pham, Taylor and Victoria all show anime work.

    Illustrative, traditional and blackwork. Aaron Casas works in illustrative and traditional, Hailey Lopez and Molly Monroe bring a playful illustrative eye to Honeygrove Studio, and Gilbert leans toward blackwork.

    Cover-ups. This is the one to ask about before anything else. Sean lists cover-ups in the bio and Minh Pham has a dedicated cover-up page on the artist’s site, while Eddy Castillo says cover-ups are not taken.

    How this list was built

    A San Antonio tattoo artist made this list only if we could read a public source that placed them in the city and described what they do. We used four checks:

    1. Location. A studio roster, personal website or Instagram bio names San Antonio, a San Antonio studio or a nearby area such as Helotes.
    2. Specialty. The source states a style, or the artist’s own posts make it obvious. Where neither is clear, the card says “style not stated” instead of guessing.
    3. Active presence. The profile showed recent posts or a current booking method when we checked on 18 October 2026.
    4. Booking terms as written. We copy deposits, minimums and open-or-closed status exactly as the artist states them and name the source.

    What we excluded. Two artists whose pages mention San Antonio list their studio location as Austin, so they are not here. Another artist’s bio did not confirm a San Antonio location, so that name was dropped too. We also did not use any image of an artist’s work: tattoo images belong to the artist and the client, so each card links to the artist’s own page instead.

    Order. The “All styles” view is alphabetical. Inside the Realism, Black & grey and Cover-ups filters, the “Recommended order” is an editorial choice by the publisher. It is not a measure of skill, so switch to “Name, A to Z” if you want a neutral order.

    Limits. This is a shortlist, not a ranking and not a census. Several entries rest on a single Instagram bio, which can change overnight, and five of the twenty come from one studio’s published roster because that studio is the only one in our search that lists each artist with a specialty. A different method would produce a different twenty.

    Questions people ask

    How were these 20 San Antonio tattoo artists chosen?

    Each artist appears in a public source we could open and read: a studio’s own artist page, the artist’s own website, or an Instagram bio that names San Antonio or a San Antonio studio. We then required a stated specialty or visible body of work, and we recorded booking details as written. The order is not a ranking of skill: “All styles” is alphabetical, and inside a style filter the order is an editorial choice by the publisher.

    Which of these artists take walk-ins?

    In the sources we checked, only Ricky Salinas Jr. states that walk-ins and appointments are both accepted. Every other entry either books by appointment, uses a waitlist or does not say. Confirm directly before you show up.

    How much is the deposit or minimum?

    Where artists publish figures, they range from a $100 minimum (Ricky Salinas Jr.) to a $200 minimum with a $50 to $100 deposit (Megan Fox), plus a $100 non-refundable deposit for Gilbert at Livewire Tattoo. Most entries publish no price at all, so treat these as examples of how artists state terms, not a price guide.

    Do tattoo studios in Texas need a license?

    Yes. The Texas Department of State Health Services licenses tattoo studios under Health and Safety Code Chapter 146. Ask to see the studio license before you book, and read the DSHS licensing requirements for what a licensed studio must do.

    Who should I follow for anime tattoos in San Antonio?

    Three entries show anime work: Minh Pham (@pham.minh.phuc), Taylor (@tsukitattoos, featured by the San Antonio Current in 2024) and Victoria (@victoriaatattoos, who pairs anime with flowers). Compare their healed work and ask how each handles color saturation and fine outlines.

    Which artists do cover-ups?

    Sean (@lechetattoos) lists cover-ups among the services in the bio, and Minh Pham (@pham.minh.phuc) has a dedicated cover-up page on the artist’s site. Eddy Castillo’s bio says cover-ups are not taken. For everyone else the sources we checked do not say, so ask during the consultation.

    Why is a well-known artist missing from the list?

    This is a verified shortlist, not a census. We left out artists whose public pages list a studio outside San Antonio, even when their marketing mentions the city, and artists whose location we could not confirm. A different method would surface a different twenty.
